# Diet, Sodium, and Protein Guidance for Cirrhosis

Source: AASLD Practice Guidance on malnutrition, frailty, and sarcopenia in cirrhosis, patient-summary version (guidelinecentral.com/guideline/333898/patient-summary/1999861/), condensed for MedAgent RAG index. Flagged as a known gap in earlier corpus notes; addresses the "diet/sodium guidance" item specifically.

## Why nutrition matters in cirrhosis

Malnutrition and muscle loss (sarcopenia) are common in cirrhosis and are linked to worse outcomes — a damaged liver is less efficient at storing energy, so the body turns to breaking down muscle for fuel more readily than in a healthy person, especially overnight. This makes eating pattern, not just food choice, clinically relevant.

## Protein: more is usually needed, not less

A common misconception is that liver disease means restricting protein — this is outdated and, per current AASLD guidance, actively harmful for most patients. The recommended intake for adults with cirrhosis is **1.2-1.5 g of protein per kg of ideal body weight per day** (higher, 1.5 g/kg, for patients who are also sarcopenic) — meaningfully more than the general healthy-adult recommendation. Protein should be spread across varied sources (including vegetable and dairy protein, not just meat). Critically, **protein should not be restricted even in patients with hepatic encephalopathy** — the older practice of protein restriction for HE has been superseded; adequate protein intake actually supports recovery, while inadequate intake accelerates muscle loss.

## Sodium: restriction is about fluid retention, not liver disease itself

Sodium restriction in cirrhosis exists specifically to manage **fluid retention — ascites and edema** (see `02_kz_protocol_cirrhosis.md` for ascites-specific sodium targets, roughly 80-120 mmol/day, and diuretic dosing by ascites grade). Current AASLD guidance frames sodium restriction as appropriate when a patient has clinically significant fluid retention, not as a blanket rule for every cirrhosis patient regardless of fluid status. Importantly, if strict sodium restriction is making it hard for a patient to eat enough overall (a real risk in someone already at risk of malnutrition or frailty), guidance supports *relaxing* the sodium restriction somewhat rather than letting overall nutrition suffer — the goal is balancing ascites control against adequate food intake, not sodium restriction as an end in itself.

## Meal timing: avoid long fasting gaps

Because cirrhotic livers have reduced glycogen reserves, going long stretches without eating pushes the body toward muscle breakdown sooner than it would in someone with a healthy liver. Recommended pattern: no more than about 3-4 hours between food intake while awake, plus a small early breakfast and a late-evening snack — the overnight fast is the single longest gap in most people's day, so a bedtime snack specifically helps offset overnight muscle breakdown.

## Practical takeaways

- Eating enough — including enough protein — matters more than avoiding protein.
- Sodium limits are targeted at fluid retention (ascites/edema), and are adjusted individually rather than applied as a fixed universal rule.
- Small, frequent meals/snacks (including a bedtime snack) help protect muscle mass.
- Micronutrient deficiencies (including fat-soluble vitamins, relevant given PBC's cholestasis — see `13_bone_health_pbc.md`) are worth checking periodically; a physician or dietitian can screen for and correct these.
