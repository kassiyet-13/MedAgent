# Pruritus (Itching) in PBC and Cholestatic Liver Disease

Source: AASLD Liver Fellow Network clinical pearls (aasld.org/liver-fellow-network/core-series/clinical-pearls/scratching-itch-management-pruritus-cholestatic) and the Kazakhstan MoH PBC/PSC clinical protocol (Protocol No. 35, 2017, diseases.medelement.com — same source as `09_pbc_second_line_therapy.md`), condensed for MedAgent RAG index.

## Why pruritus happens in PBC

Pruritus (кожный зуд) is one of the most common and quality-of-life-affecting symptoms of PBC, caused by the buildup of bile acids and other substances that would normally be cleared through bile but instead accumulate due to cholestasis. It is already mentioned as a general cirrhosis symptom in `03_cirrhosis_patient_overview.md`; this file covers PBC-specific, stepwise management. It can occur independent of disease severity — even patients with well-controlled liver biochemistry can have significant itch — and it commonly worsens at night, with heat, or with certain fabrics.

## Non-drug measures (first, low-risk step)

Simple measures that can help regardless of what medication step a patient is on: emollients/moisturizers, avoiding very hot baths/showers, keeping fingernails trimmed short (to limit skin damage from scratching), loose/breathable clothing, and avoiding known personal triggers.

## Stepwise medication approach

Both sources describe a similar stepwise (first-line → next-line) approach; specific drugs/doses below are drawn from the KZ PBC protocol tables, consistent with the AASLD summary's ordering:

1. **Cholestyramine** (a bile-acid-binding resin) — first-line. Typical dose range 4-16 g/day. Important practical point emphasized in both sources: it must be taken separately from other medications, including UDCA — roughly 1 hour before or 4 hours after other drugs (KZ protocol specifies 3-4 hours around UDCA specifically) — because it can bind and block absorption of other oral medications if taken together. Common downsides are GI upset and poor taste, which affect adherence.
2. **Rifampicin** — second-line, for patients not adequately helped by cholestyramine. Typical dose range roughly 150-600 mg/day (sources vary somewhat: AASLD summary cites 150-300 mg twice daily). Carries a risk of drug-induced liver injury and should be used cautiously, with monitoring, particularly in patients with higher bilirubin.
3. **Naltrexone** (an opioid antagonist) — third-line. Started at a low dose and increased gradually, because starting at a full dose can trigger opioid-withdrawal-like symptoms in patients whose bodies have adapted to elevated endogenous opioid tone from chronic itch.
4. **Sertraline** (an SSRI antidepressant, used off-label here for itch, not depression) or **gabapentin** — later-line adjunctive options with a relatively favorable side-effect profile, used when earlier steps are insufficient.
5. **Bezafibrate** — notably, in patients already on obeticholic acid (OCA) who develop or have worsened pruritus (a known OCA side effect, see `09_pbc_second_line_therapy.md`), both bezafibrate and rifampicin have evidence for reducing itch intensity; if control is still insufficient after about 4 weeks or side effects occur, naltrexone can be added.
6. **Refractory cases**: for itch that doesn't respond to the above, options include ultraviolet B phototherapy (dermatology referral) or, rarely, more invasive approaches (e.g., albumin dialysis) — these are specialist-level decisions.

## Application notes for MedAgent

- This is a symptom directly relevant to quality of life for this patient (PBC, on obeticholic acid — a drug whose most common side effect is pruritus). The app can explain what each step of therapy is and why cholestyramine needs to be spaced apart from other medications (a genuinely actionable, safe piece of information), but must not suggest starting, stopping, or escalating any specific itch medication — that is a physician decision based on where the patient currently sits in this stepwise ladder.
- If the patient reports new or worsening itch in a follow-up chat, the app's role is to acknowledge it, offer the plain-language "why this happens" explanation, and suggest mentioning it to her care team (especially useful context: itch can be an OCA side effect, not necessarily disease worsening) — not to suggest an over-the-counter remedy beyond the general non-drug measures above.
