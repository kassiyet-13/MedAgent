# PBC Second-Line Therapy: Obeticholic Acid and Fibrates

Source: APASL clinical practice guidance summary (PMC8843914, same source as `01_pbc_diagnosis_udca_response.md`) plus the Kazakhstan MoH clinical protocol specifically for PBC/PSC (Protocol No. 35, approved 14 December 2017, diseases.medelement.com) — a PBC-specific KZ protocol that was an open action item in earlier corpus notes and has now been located. Condensed for MedAgent RAG index.

## When second-line therapy comes up

As covered in `01_pbc_diagnosis_udca_response.md`, roughly 30-40% of patients on UDCA show an insufficient biochemical response at 6-12 months by criteria like Paris-II (ALP and AST ≤1.5x ULN plus normal bilirubin) or the GLOBE score. For these patients — and for the small number who cannot tolerate UDCA — guidelines describe second-line add-on therapy. Obeticholic acid (OCA) is the only agent specifically approved as second-line PBC therapy for UDCA non-responders; fibrates (bezafibrate, fenofibrate) are used off-label/adjunctively with growing trial evidence. **Whether and when to start second-line therapy is a physician decision** based on the full biochemical response picture — this file explains what the medicines are and do, not a recommendation to use them.

## Obeticholic acid (OCA / "Обетимус" and other brand names)

OCA is a semi-synthetic bile acid analog that activates the farnesoid X receptor (FXR), a pathway involved in bile acid regulation.

- **Dosing**: started at 5 mg/day, and — per both the APASL summary and the KZ PBC protocol — increased to 10 mg/day after around 6 months if tolerated and needed, titrated by the treating physician based on response and tolerability.
- **Efficacy**: in the phase III POISE trial, roughly half of prior UDCA non-responders/intolerant patients achieved a defined biochemical response (ALP and bilirubin improvement) versus placebo, with benefit sustained through longer follow-up.
- **Important safety restriction**: OCA carries an FDA warning against use in PBC patients with decompensated cirrhosis (Child-Pugh-Turcotte class B or C) — use in this group has been associated with serious liver-related harm. It is intended for non-cirrhotic patients or those with compensated (Child-Pugh-Turcotte class A) cirrhosis only. This makes a patient's CTP class (see `02_kz_protocol_cirrhosis.md`) directly relevant to whether OCA is an appropriate option — again, a determination for the treating physician, not this app.
- **Pruritus as a side effect**: itching is a common, dose-related side effect of OCA — in the POISE trial the majority of OCA-treated patients reported some pruritus, and it was a common reason for dose reduction or discontinuation. See `10_pruritus_cholestasis_management.md` for how this is managed when it occurs.

## Fibrates (bezafibrate, fenofibrate)

Fibrates (normally used as cholesterol-lowering drugs) also improve cholestatic liver biochemistry in PBC when added to UDCA, and are used off-label for this purpose in many countries.

- **Dosing** cited in both sources: bezafibrate 400 mg/day, or fenofibrate ~145-200 mg/day.
- **Efficacy**: trial evidence (including a 2018 phase III bezafibrate trial) shows meaningful reductions in ALP, GGT, and other cholestatic markers when added to UDCA, with a higher rate of "complete biochemical response" than UDCA alone in inadequate responders.
- **Pruritus benefit**: unlike OCA, fibrates (bezafibrate in particular, studied in the FITCH trial across PBC and PSC) tend to *reduce* itch — around 45-55% of patients with moderate-to-severe pruritus had a meaningful reduction in itch intensity on bezafibrate versus placebo. This makes fibrates a reasonable option specifically when pruritus is already a problem.
- **Safety monitoring**: fibrate-UDCA combination therapy is associated with more treatment-related side effects than UDCA alone, including rises in serum creatinine and muscle pain (myalgia) — monitoring is particularly emphasized in cirrhotic patients.
- Combination OCA + fibrate ("triple therapy" with UDCA) has been studied in difficult-to-treat non-responders and shows an additive normalization effect on liver biochemistry, at the cost of needing to watch for both agents' respective side effects.

## Application notes for MedAgent

- This file is directly relevant to the patient's real-world regimen (obeticholic acid, "Обетимус," appears on her actual medication list) — the app can use this content to explain what the medication is/does and why it may have been added, but must not comment on whether it is the "right" choice for her specific case, nor suggest dose changes.
- If the patient's discharge summary or medication list shows both a cirrhosis diagnosis and OCA use, the app should not draw conclusions about her CTP class from that alone — CTP class determination is a clinical assessment, not something to infer and state back to the patient.
- Cross-reference `01_pbc_diagnosis_udca_response.md` for the underlying UDCA-response criteria that triggers consideration of second-line therapy, and `10_pruritus_cholestasis_management.md` for itch management if OCA-related pruritus comes up.
