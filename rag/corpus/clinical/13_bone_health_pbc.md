# Bone Health in PBC: Osteoporosis and Osteopenia

Source: PMC review "Primary Biliary Cholangitis-associated Osteoporosis: Contemporary Review of Pathogenesis and Management" (pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12872389/) and the Kazakhstan MoH PBC/PSC clinical protocol monitoring schedule (Protocol No. 35, 2017 — same source as `09_pbc_second_line_therapy.md`), condensed for MedAgent RAG index.

## Why PBC affects bone health

Metabolic bone disease (reduced bone density — osteopenia and, if more advanced, osteoporosis) is common in PBC. Reported prevalence varies by study and region but is substantial — meta-analysis figures cited around 38% osteoporosis and 39% osteopenia among PBC patients, with country-level estimates ranging roughly 20-45%. Several mechanisms contribute, more about impaired bone *formation* than increased breakdown:

- Chronic cholestasis leads to malabsorption of fat-soluble vitamins, including vitamin D (and vitamin K), both important for bone mineralization.
- Bile acid and bilirubin buildup itself appears to directly inhibit the bone-building cells (osteoblasts).
- Immune/inflammatory activity typical of PBC (elevated inflammatory signaling) also promotes bone breakdown pathways.
- General risk factors that compound this: older age, longer disease duration, lower body weight, more advanced liver fibrosis, and reduced muscle mass (sarcopenia).

## Screening

Both sources support baseline and periodic monitoring rather than a one-time check:

- **Bone mineral density (DEXA scan)** — recommended at PBC diagnosis as a baseline, then rechecked periodically; the KZ PBC protocol specifies **every 2 years**. A T-score of -2.5 or lower defines osteoporosis by standard DEXA criteria (the same general threshold used outside liver disease).
- **Vitamin D level** — checked periodically (annually, per the KZ protocol's broader monitoring schedule for fat-soluble vitamins) given the malabsorption risk described above.
- **Calcium level** — checked alongside vitamin D as part of the same monitoring picture.

## Supplementation and treatment

- General guidance for patients at risk cites daily intake targets in the range of roughly **1,000-1,200 mg calcium** and **around 800 IU vitamin D** — consistent with the patient's own medication list already including calcium and vitamin D supplementation, which fits this preventive/maintenance role.
- For patients who already have osteopenia or osteoporosis on DEXA, treatment may extend beyond calcium/vitamin D to include bone-specific medications (e.g., bisphosphonates), decided by the treating physician based on DEXA severity and individual risk factors — evidence specifically in PBC patients for some of these agents is still described as limited/mixed in the literature, so this is very much an individualized decision rather than an automatic next step.
