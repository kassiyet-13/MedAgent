# Cirrhosis: Patient-Friendly Overview

Source: MedlinePlus (medlineplus.gov/cirrhosis.html), U.S. National Library of Medicine — public domain, condensed for MedAgent RAG index.

## What is cirrhosis?

Cirrhosis is scarring of the liver caused by long-term injury or disease. Scar tissue cannot do what healthy liver tissue does — make proteins, help fight infections, clean the blood, help digest food, and store energy.

## Common causes

Chronic alcohol use and viral hepatitis are the most common causes generally. Other causes include nonalcoholic fatty liver disease and autoimmune conditions such as primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) — the relevant cause for this app's target user.

## Symptoms and complications to watch for

- Easy bruising, bleeding, or nosebleeds
- Swelling in the abdomen or legs (ascites/edema)
- Increased sensitivity to medications
- High blood pressure in the veins entering the liver (portal hypertension)
- Enlarged veins (varices) in the esophagus/stomach that can bleed suddenly — a medical emergency
- Kidney problems
- Yellowing of skin/eyes (jaundice)
- Severe itching (common in PBC specifically, due to bile acid buildup)
- Gallstones
- A small proportion of patients develop liver cancer (hence routine AFP + imaging surveillance)

## How it's diagnosed and monitored

Blood tests, imaging (ultrasound, CT, MRI, FibroScan), and occasionally liver biopsy.

## Managing cirrhosis

Scar tissue itself cannot be reversed, but treating the underlying cause (e.g. UDCA for PBC) can keep it from getting worse. In advanced disease, liver transplant may become necessary. Day-to-day management focuses on: limiting salt (for ascites), avoiding alcohol and unnecessary medications that stress the liver, staying up to date on vaccinations, regular monitoring, and prompt attention to warning signs like confusion, black/bloody stools, or rapidly increasing abdominal swelling.
